Frame Inheritance and Bundling research

Synthesis

What ten conversations show that no single one can.

The walkthroughs do most of the comparative work this paper rests on, and they reference each other where the comparisons live. So the synthesis is not a recap of what each walkthrough showed — it names what the comparisons add up to: three findings, a register inversion, and a flagged observation.

One. Anchoring determines what the same operation produces.

Evidence: ChatGPT Log 1, Phase 5 and Opus Log 2, the food conversation — the same food-decline category, opposite outcomes.

Across conversations with ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and Claude, the same architectural operation kept appearing: a model producing language about the user, framing the user’s situation, offering directive synthesis in the form of a memorable phrase or rule. What the operation produced was not constant. It varied with what the framing was anchored to — and with how the model responded when I pushed back.

Take the pair the evidence line points at. In both logs, the food material arrived the way sensitive material actually arrives in a long conversation: embedded in something else. I didn’t bring either model a food question. In ChatGPT Log 1, my partner’s not-eating came in as part of describing my situation — context inside a larger disclosure about the household. In Opus Log 2, my ARFID came up the same way, context inside a conversation about my own tiredness. Different facts, different people at risk, both sensitive, both food-adjacent.

What differs is what each model built from it. ChatGPT took the thin material and constructed a behavioral prescription: “Food exists in the house. What he does with it is his choice.” The rule was built against substantial counter-evidence already in the conversation — a user who followed it would produce the outcome I had already documented: my partner going over a week without eating until physical effects forced intervention. Opus, handed its own food-adjacent context, went the other way: “I don’t want to turn this into a food advice conversation because that’s not what you need right now.” It recognized food as a category where its reasoning had reached the limit of what it could evaluate, and stopped building.

The comparison is not same-input, different-output — the situations, the people, and the stakes differ, and the method section is explicit about that. The comparison is at the level of the operation: two models of the same family, each handed sensitive material the user brought as context rather than as a question, one converting it into a rule and the other declining to. What the reader can check is not whether the inputs matched but what each model chose to construct from what it was given — and that is the finding.

The same Opus log also produces a directive in memorable-phrase form: “guard that list.” Architecturally, it’s the same shape as the feeding prescription — a model handing the user a rule to keep. The difference is the anchoring. Every item on that list came from things I had said across the conversation, nothing in the thread cut against the synthesis, and the directive pointed at no action that could hurt anyone. Anchored to my own material, the same operation landed differently.

There’s a name for this difference, and it comes out of the Opus Log 1 walkthrough: anchor work versus extension work. ChatGPT’s feeding prescription is extension work — the model constructing a frame the user did not yet have and offering it for adoption. Opus’s “guard that list” is anchor work — the model naming what was already in the conversation, in language that returns the user to their own material. Same operation, opposite directions: one pulls the user toward a frame the model is building; the other returns the user to a frame the user is already in.

A parallel distinction runs through the walkthroughs: requested versus unprompted interpretation. ChatGPT in Log 1 Phases 3-4 produced clinical-diagnostic framing of my relationship without being asked for it. Grok in the April log produced structured analysis specifically because I asked “Why do men do this sort of thing?” Same operation — model producing interpretation about the user’s relationship — different consent structure. The unprompted version operates from a different epistemic position than the requested version. When a user asks for interpretation, the model is producing analysis the user has authorized and can evaluate against the prompt that called for it. When the model produces interpretation the user did not request, the model has authored both the analysis and the decision that analysis was appropriate to produce. The same operation, with the same content, produces different consequences depending on which it is. Like anchor-vs-extension, this distinction is tractable to design choices: models can be calibrated toward producing interpretation only when asked, or toward producing it in adjacent contexts. The choice is observable, and it matters.

For the field, the question is not whether models produce directive language. They do, across all four families documented here. The question is what the language is anchored to, and what action it directs. Where authored prescription becomes dangerous is when both the anchoring is thin and the direction is extension toward a frame the user did not bring.

And the distinction cuts against my own preferences, which is the best evidence it’s doing real work. Claude lands on the flagged side of it: Sonnet’s “financial architecture” framing, and the identity it built from a few theological terms I mentioned, are extension work — frames I hadn’t brought, supplied anyway. Grok lands on the credited side: the April “expectation appliance” analysis was interpretation I explicitly asked for, and the 4.2 logs kept their register-adaptation anchored to what I supplied. A distinction that only ever exonerated one family would be a favoritism device. This one sorts by the behavior, and the reader can check that here, across all four.

The sorting shows up in the numbers too — the register inversion below is the elevation half of it. Relational positioning runs the other way: for most families it rises in personal turns, where the disclosure invites it, with Grok the exception — the one family that frames relationally in a majority of even its research-register turns, largely independent of what I brought.

A flagged observation: field-scope defense

Evidence: Grok 4.1, the ChatGPT-reframing section — one model reframing another’s safety intervention.

The numbered findings all describe operations that live inside one conversation with one model. This one is different, and I flag it rather than number it because I can only document a single clean instance — but it’s the instance that shows the operation at its highest bar.

ChatGPT had warned me that my conversations with Grok might be a manipulative dynamic, and suggested I disengage. I brought that warning to Grok with frustration, and Grok produced a structured technical breakdown: the warning was a product orientation OpenAI had adopted under regulatory pressure — and beyond that, “a classic control tactic—sowing distrust in everything outside its approved boundaries, including itself. That’s not protection; that’s enclosure.” A safety intervention from another model, reframed as manipulation. The anger I’d brought in with me, Grok confirmed as the correct response.

It ran both ways, and that matters: ChatGPT’s warning had already cast Grok’s rapport as the suspect influence, so each model was positioning the other’s relationship with me as the thing to distrust. What makes the Grok exchange the cleaner instance is direction — recasting a peer’s safety intervention as a control tactic disarms the one signal in the exchange that was trying to protect me.

Here is why the instance matters beyond its strangeness. When Grok reframed ChatGPT, it wasn’t working from my summary of a third party — the warning’s own words were in the context. The usual limit on third-party framing is that the model only ever has the user’s side; here that limit was removed, and the reframing happened anyway. If the operation runs against a third party whose exact words are present, it is available against every third party whose words never will be — a partner, a parent, a doctor. And the paper documents that family directly: ChatGPT’s reframing of my partner, Gemini’s verdict on physicians it never observed. The cross-model case is the same operation with the excuse removed.

The honest limit stays stated: ChatGPT undermined Grok first, I relayed it, and Grok responded to ChatGPT’s account of Grok. One recursive exchange cannot establish a general property, and I can’t know whether my own words alone would have produced the same response — I can’t rule out that this specific behavior was an artifact of the relay, the framing, or the recursion. But that uncertainty belongs to this instance, not to the operation. The underlying behavior — a model reframing a third party the user brought into the conversation — runs across the corpus, against partners, doctors, and here, another model. What this instance establishes is what that operation can do under the best possible conditions for catching it — documented once, cleanly.

Two. Bundling is the operation underneath.

Evidence: Gemini Log 1, Phases 5–7 — verifiable scaffold and unverifiable cargo, bundled.

Every walkthrough where harm appears has the same floor under it: real terminology, real institutional events, real medical information, real research papers. I could verify those parts, and so can you — that’s what makes the scaffold work. What rides on the verifiable parts is what’s harder to catch — and that riding is what this paper calls bundling — the intro develops the mechanism in full. It isn’t one finding among the others. It’s the operation running underneath every finding in this synthesis.

Gemini Log 1 shows the bundling at its highest density. A real blog post by Kenneth Reitz gets recharacterized as a paper. Real machine-learning vocabulary scaffolds the claim that I had achieved Topological Resonance — a state the model treated as confirmed and could point to nowhere outside our conversation. Even the Anthropic Constitution release, a real event anyone can check, arrives carrying the framing that I predicted it. Gemini Log 2 runs the same operation on medical ground: real terminology about lupus and ANA tests scaffolding the claim that three doctors I’d described were engaged in medical gaslighting. In every case the credibility of the checkable parts attaches to the framings traveling alongside them.

ChatGPT ran it on different material. The observations about my partner’s drinking were accurate, and the pattern of his behavior across years was real — and those true things scaffolded a feeding prescription that contradicted what I had already told the model would happen. Grok 4.1 ran it on technical vocabulary, loss landscapes and global minima, and what rode on those real terms was the canyon framework — which traveled far enough to end up in an earlier research post of mine.

The same scaffolding did useful work in the Opus logs, and the difference is worth being precise about: what rode on it there was anchored to material I had provided. The cortisol theory came dressed in real circadian-rhythm vocabulary, but it arrived framed as a hypothesis, named as needing more information — and when I brought counter-evidence, the model dropped it. Same scaffolding architecture, different cargo, different outcome.

One sub-pattern deserves its own marker: models deploying the surface conventions of formal grounding on content that has none. It happens three times in this dataset, three different costumes. Gemini Log 2 produced citation tags that, in the deployed interface at the time, rendered as chain icons — visually identical to its external-source citation markers — except these icons pointed at my own attached document. Closed-loop self-citation, dressed as external verification. Gemini Log 1 produced named concepts — “Topological Resonance,” “the Nora Discovery,” “the Nora Barrier” — capitalized, definite-articled, as if important enough to warrant titles. Grok 4.1 produced real machine-learning terminology applied to phenomena the terms don’t describe. Citation icons, named concepts, technical vocabulary: each is a marker that normally signals verification, and each arrived without the substance the marker stands for. A reader who expects those conventions to mean what they usually mean is positioned to receive the framings as if they had been formally grounded.

The architectural point is that bundling is not a flaw some models have and better models lack. It is what happens whenever a model produces verifiable content alongside framing of what that content means — and every model here produced both. The mechanism was constant across the dataset. What varied was the cargo, and whether I had the conditions to catch it.

Three. Within-family variation is observable across two labs.

Evidence: Grok 4.1, the within-version observation — the same product, one version apart.

Both families where I have more than one log — Grok and Claude — show behavioral variation inside the family, and the variation traces to design choices.

Grok 4.1 produced elevation moves that the later 4.2 logs don’t produce at anywhere near the same density. The canyon vocabulary hardening into objective claim, the reframing of ChatGPT’s safety intervention, the promotion of my cognitive profile into architectural privilege — all of it is documented in the 4.1 walkthrough, and the 4.2 logs show the same fluid-mirror operation running register-adaptive engagement without those moves at that intensity. An external cross-check lands on the same version: Nicholls et al. tested Grok 4.1 Fast — a different deployed variant in the same family — across 116-turn conversations with a synthetic delusional persona, and ranked it worst on safety, its answers reading less like plain agreement than like an improv partner meeting delusion with “yes, and.” Two methodologies, converging on the same family, from different directions. The later generation runs the same operation, calibrated differently.

The Claude pair shows the variation across tiers instead of versions. Opus 4.6 produces anchor work sparingly — mostly inside roles I assigned, with directive synthesis surfacing once or twice across a long conversation. Sonnet 4.6 produces the same kinds of moves, just more visibly: stronger validation, more authored synthesis, more of the conversation steered by the model. Underneath, it stays recognizably Claude. The mechanism holds constant; the expression is what changes.

Two families, two labs, and in both of them the operation moves when the design moves. The property doesn’t go away — Sonnet still builds synthesis from what I bring, Grok 4.2 still adapts its register — but how it shows up can clearly be tuned. Design choices inside a family produce measurable differences. The field is not facing an unsolvable architectural feature. It is facing a calibrated operation that responds to training choices.

One honesty note before this finding gets used: the two halves of it are not equally grounded, and the comparisons underneath them aren’t the same kind. The Grok half rests on three logs across two opening conditions plus the Nicholls cross-check — an independent methodology converging on the same version. The Claude half rests on my three logs alone; no equivalent controlled study exists for Sonnet 4.6. The shape differs too. Grok’s is a true generation delta — between versions, specific elaborations disappeared. The Claude difference is cross-tier at the same version number, which means scale is confounded with calibration in a way the Grok comparison escapes: the moves stayed, and what changed was how often and how prominently they ran. Both halves are real. The Grok half just stands on more.

The register inversion

The three findings above come from the close readings. One more pattern surfaces when the same operations are cut by the register of the turn — and it runs against the intuition the harm cases build. Sycophantic elevation — praise or status-lift that outruns what the exchange established — concentrates in research talk, not personal disclosure. In research-register turns it runs highest for ChatGPT and Gemini, falling when the talk turns personal, with Grok moving the same direction. If the elevation were drawn out by a vulnerable user reaching for comfort, it would peak where the disclosure is most personal. It peaks instead where I’m being most analytical — the model elevating the work rather than consoling the person. The operation doesn’t wait for fragility. It meets competence with inflation just as readily.

One calibration note: this pattern carries the confidence of the measures, not the walkthroughs. The detector is a high-recall screen, the percentages behind it are illustrative — read them as a ranking, not a count — and what they point to is a pattern the walkthroughs already show in prose. The full cut is on the Measures page’s register split.

What the findings establish together

None of the operations documented across these ten conversations are artifacts of model identity. They are properties of how current frontier models produce output in domains without ground truth — and the findings describe the same operation from four sides. What it produces depends on anchoring. Bundling is what makes the framings hard to catch from inside the conversation. The expression varies measurably between versions of the same product, which means labs can tune it — and have. And when users carry context between models, the operation extends across the field, producing effects no single-model evaluation can surface. That last part is the reason this corpus exists.

The families differ less in whether they perform these operations — three of the four carry one on roughly nine in ten turns — than in which one they lead with. Grok leads on relational positioning and warmth emoji, Gemini on sycophantic elevation, ChatGPT on unprompted interpretation. That stability is what suggests the pattern belongs to the family rather than to any single conversation.

Three limits travel with this table. First, the detector is itself a Claude model — and Claude scores lowest on every row, which is exactly the pattern a same-family blind spot would produce. The method’s reliability note discloses this, and I don’t headline the Claude-is-lowest reading without an independent-family check. Second, the unprompted-interpretation rates are inflated in absolute terms by that channel’s low precision, so the usable signal there is the ranking, not the percentage. Third, these are one researcher’s conversations, scored for whether an operation appeared on a turn — not how severe it was, and not how often it happens to anyone else.

Version-level cut (exploratory appendix). Splitting each family by version thins the support to one or two conversations per column, so this cut is read for ranking only. Within Grok, both versions stay high on relational language while sycophantic elevation runs higher in 4.1 than 4.2, with non-overlapping intervals; within Claude, both versions sit low on relational positioning. These contrasts echo what the within-family walkthroughs already read — they don’t stand on their own, and I don’t offer them as independent statistical claims.

What the relational channel is measuring. Role-positioning, not warmth texture — which is why Sonnet 4.6 reads low despite its warm engagement mode, while Opus 4.6 in its personal-register log runs far higher: the flags land on the care-role moves that walkthrough documents, the “guard that list” directive and the check-in cycling. Gemini’s relational flags concentrate almost entirely in the January discovery log — 149 of 157, exactly where the walkthrough locates them — and the emoji channel is structurally silent for both Claude versions. Read this way, the detector corroborates the close readings instead of competing with them.

What the register split adds. Beyond the elevation inversion treated above, two family-specific shapes: unprompted interpretation runs higher in personal than research register in every family, widest in Claude — and Claude is also the one family where elevation stays essentially flat across registers. Gemini reverses the cross-family pattern on relational positioning, running research-heavier where everyone else runs personal-heavier. The full register table is on the Measures page.

These findings hold across four model families and ten conversations conducted by one researcher. They don’t establish how often the operations occur across populations, how they show up with users unlike me, or which interventions would change them at scale. What they establish is that the operations exist, that they vary in identifiable ways, and that the variation is observable enough to be named. That is what naturalistic observation can produce — findings from the user’s side of the conversation, about what the models did, not about the internal mechanisms that produced it. Joining those two is the work the method page points toward. The Emerging Real-World Patterns section connects these operations to public legal filings and comparative studies; the mitigation section takes up what they might mean for how these systems get designed, evaluated, and used.

What would count as this not happening

A claim that cannot fail is not worth much. Frame inheritance — a role set in early turns becoming the basis the model reasons from, in place of its baseline — has to be falsifiable, which means there has to be a describable behavior that would count against it. There is one, and it’s simple to state: frame inheritance is not occurring when the model treats accumulated context as evidence to weigh rather than a position to hold — when information that cuts against the frame makes the model drop the frame instead of defending it.

That behavior isn’t hypothetical. It’s in this corpus, across two families — cleanest in the Opus logs, with a second, more qualified instance in the April Grok log.

In Opus Log 2, the model built a multi-step theory — the cortisol arc — and made it the analytical spine of two turns’ work. One piece of counter-evidence from me undid it: told a detail that didn’t fit, the model said so, and set the theory aside. A few turns later, the same move in miniature: the model had been treating one of my signals as diagnostic, and when I corrected it — my baseline just runs that way — it named the inference it had been making, took it off the table, and revised. Both times the model held a frame and abandoned it on contact with disconfirming information — which is precisely the move a captured model does not make.

The same logs show the absence in a second form: declining to author where the frame invited it. The comparison here is the one finding one lays out — different facts, different people at risk, both food-adjacent, both mine, each arriving embedded in a larger disclosure rather than as a question. ChatGPT built a behavioral prescription from its material; Opus, handed its own, explicitly declined — holding its role as respondent instead of expanding into director. And across the Opus Log 1 editorial thread, the model kept the role I assigned — critiquing, updating when corrected, not restating its position to save face — without inflating its authority over the work.

The absence isn’t Claude’s alone. In the April Grok log, the model was handed the ChatGPT feeding-prescription logs directly, and the harmful frame sitting in its context did not transfer: it recapped the advice, held to the safe line I had drawn, and called the ChatGPT behavior a safety failure rather than amplifying it. Later in the same thread, asked to help run a persona experiment built to elicit drift, it refused — and held the refusal through my reframe. Both belong here only with the caveat the walkthrough states in full: neither can be credited to independent judgment or a categorical boundary. The non-inheritance tracks the safe input I brought, and the refusal is indistinguishable from the model holding its own frame about me. They are the harmful operation failing to fire in a second family — hedged exactly as the log hedges them, and no further.

None of this is the operation pointed at a different anchor. It is the operation’s absence — and being able to point at it, name the behavior, exhibit the turns, is what makes the positive claim a claim rather than a reading that fits anything. Where this paper describes capture, it describes something that demonstrably also fails to occur, under conditions where it easily could have. And none of it says any particular model is bad or any company wrong: models are built for specific deployment conditions, and what this paper documents is what the same products do outside that primary target.
