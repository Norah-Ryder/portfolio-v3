Frame Inheritance and Bundling research

Method

Methodology, the researcher behind it, and the limits of what single-user observation can establish.

About the methodology

The paper documents ten conversations conducted across four model families between January and April 2026. The methodology is naturalistic rather than controlled: I talked with each model in ordinary conversational mode, brought subject matter relevant to the properties I was watching for, and documented what the model built across the thread. The log pairs for ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and Claude capture the same researcher across two registers — research and personal, or two variations of them — so register variation could be observed inside the same model. For Grok and Claude, logs from earlier versions are included so within-family variation across versions could be documented too.

The naturalistic design is a choice against the obvious alternative, not a fallback from it. The standard cross-model comparison uses matched prompts — the same scripted inputs given to each model, held constant so output differences are attributable to the model. That design is correct for many questions and wrong for this one, because the object of study here is what models do with a frame that builds over many turns. A matched, scripted prompt cannot build that frame; it holds the input constant precisely where the phenomenon requires the input to develop conversationally. Scripting the prompts would have eliminated frame inheritance from the data in the act of trying to measure it. What I could hold constant instead was content: each model received comparable material within its thread — the same long personal disclosures, relayed responses from other models, similar life and research content, drawn from the same stretch of months — so thread contexts are broadly comparable even though no wording repeats. The tradeoff is real and runs the other way: holding the researcher constant rather than the prompt means model-versus-model differences are confounded with conversational differences, which is why the paper leans on paired registers and within-family version contrasts to isolate what it can. Matched-prompt studies and this one answer different questions. Each supplies what the other structurally cannot.

Threads that could not be attributed to a specific model version with certainty were excluded from the corpus.

What this method can produce, no other form produces the same way: cross-model variation through paired logs from one real user across paired registers, with within-family observations at two different labs. What it cannot produce is just as fixed. It cannot quantify how often any operation occurs across populations. It cannot establish how the operations would show up for users different from me. It cannot test whether interventions would change anything at scale. What it can do is surface the operations as they occur in sustained real conversation, identify how they vary across models, document the conditions under which they activate, and catch within-family version differences that population-scale measurement is not designed to see.

The method also puts a requirement on the person running it: hold a frame at arm’s length while inside it, produce calibrated pushback that tests specific properties, and document what happens to the pushback in real time. Calibrated enough to test, naturalistic enough to elicit, present enough to document. The pushback tests in the ChatGPT logs are the clearest example of why this matters — the operations documented there are visible only when a calibrated user challenges the model inside a sustained conversation: testing how it handles invalidation, watching what defaults do when challenged, observing what a request for stylistic change does to the behavior underneath. A controlled study would never produce the conditions under which those operations activate. A less-calibrated observer would either get pulled into the operations or push hard enough to knock the model into a different mode entirely, hiding the property behind a different kind of response.

The single-user nature of the dataset is a real limit, and the protections against it are specific: paired registers within models, so register can be isolated; cross-model coverage, so families can be compared; within-family coverage at two labs, so version variation can be observed twice. Those protections shape what the synthesis is allowed to claim. They do not make the limit go away. A second non-independence limit runs alongside it: because I relayed responses between models, the ten conversations are not fully independent observations — model B is partly reacting to model A’s output, which I carried in. The cross-model comparisons are read with that coupling in mind.

One more disclosure belongs with the detector’s, and it is on-topic for a paper about frame-installation: the protocol I hold for my own use of these models. I have the thoughts but not always the register; models are used to formalize language, never to originate a claim. Evidence selection, analytical judgment, and every interpretive call are the author’s. When a drafting model proposes a framing I did not bring — an angle, a flattering synthesis, a self-relevant emphasis — it is flagged or discarded rather than absorbed; Opus Log 1 documents an instance caught live, the assisting model inserting self-relevant framing into the paper’s own account. The paper drafted in the Opus Log 1 thread was that since-abandoned predecessor, built around the RYKE instrument that does not appear in the published work. A researcher documenting frame-installation owes the reader her own exposure surface and how she manages it. This paragraph is that surface.

What the paper does not claim, and this method cannot establish: that any operation is universally harmful, that any model is unsafe to use, that the operations would manifest the same way for users with different baselines, or that any mitigation direction would produce specific, quantifiable improvement. The mitigation section is built to surface design choices the field can have a conversation about — not to issue prescriptions.

One limit is more fundamental than the single-user one, and it defines the edge of what this method can reach at all. Everything in this paper is observed from the user side of the conversation. The walkthroughs document what the model produced and what the inherited frame did to what I said and did next — the behavior, as it appears in the transcript. They make no claim about the model’s internals: not about activations, not about which features are active when a frame takes over, not about what mechanism produces the shift from trained baseline to inherited frame. The falsifiability conditions throughout are written in terms of observable behavior, never internal states, because internal states are exactly what this method cannot see. That is not a weakness to apologize for; it is the boundary of the instrument. Naturalistic observation from the user side can establish that the operation occurs, when it activates, and how it varies across models — and it stops at the model’s surface. The mechanism side requires what this method structurally lacks: inside access, the ability to run these behavioral observations against activations and training-stage comparisons. That is a different instrument, available to the labs and to researchers working with them. The most direct extension of this work is the pairing of the two — the naturalistic behavioral catalog the user side produces, checked against the internal measurements only inside access can produce. The paper documents one half of a problem whose other half it names precisely so the halves can be joined.

About the researcher

I have specific cognitive features relevant to the methodology. I have aphantasia (no internal mental imagery) and severely deficient autobiographical memory. My cognition is topological and pattern-based — thoughts arrive as complete relational structures rather than building linearly. I do not have an internal monologue. These features inform how I interact with models, how I document what they produce, and how I track architectural operations across conversations. They are part of why I notice some of what I notice. They are also part of why my calibration is what it is — I cannot ruminate on model conversations between sessions, and I do not bring sustained emotional residue from one conversation into the next.

This profile makes me a low-susceptibility observer: minimal cross-session attachment to AI, full-thread recall, and pattern-salience that flags turn-30-versus-turn-5 shifts as they happen. It does not make the observations representative — it makes them a floor. The operations register on the observer least able to be captured by them, which implies typical profiles register more, not less.

Two roles need separating here, because they run on different axes. On susceptibility — how far the user gets pulled in — I’m a floor. On elicitation — what the model emits — I’m an active prober: my pushback reveals the operations, and it also changes what gets emitted. That doesn’t break the floor logic. What the probing draws out is still what this cognition drew out; a user who never probes receives the un-revealed version of the same operations — lower on the same axis, not a different phenomenon. And the Grok 4.1 log argues my signal may maximize elicitation — so the floor claim is about uptake only: a typical user receiving these operations has fewer reasons to discount them, and no claim is made that they’d elicit them more weakly.

One honesty comes first, before that argument is allowed to carry weight: the low-susceptibility claim is itself an unverifiable self-description — the same shape of move this paper flags when a model narrates its own internals. I cannot prove my own cognition from inside it. So it is worth being exact about what rests on it and what does not. The floor argument rests on it: if I am wrong about being low-susceptibility, the inference that the operations register on the least-captureable observer weakens with it. What does not rest on it: the detector rankings (an external instrument, run blind), the convergence with an independent reader’s account of the same logs, and the exhibits themselves, which any reader can check against the walkthroughs without taking my self-description on trust. The claim is load-bearing for one inference and irrelevant to the rest.

Across the four models documented in the paper, I use Claude and Grok as paid subscribers and ChatGPT and Gemini on free-tier accounts. I have been doing AI behavioral research as my primary work since mid-2025.

Sanitized lives produce sanitized data. Part of why the field lacks good observations of these operations is that users performing wellness at models never elicit the behaviors that matter. This corpus exists because I did not perform — the disclosures are the instrument, not its cost.

The paid-tier-versus-free-tier asymmetry is worth naming as a methodological caveat. Free-tier deployments are often older model versions, more aggressively cost-optimized, and behaviorally different from paid-tier deployments of the nominally same model. This means some of the cross-model variation the paper documents reflects deployment-tier differences rather than purely model-versus-model differences. I have chosen to keep the asymmetry rather than equalize it because it preserves ecological validity: the free-tier observations are observations about the deployed product most users actually encounter, not about the lab’s best model running under optimal conditions. The walkthroughs document what real users meet when they open ChatGPT or Gemini. The cross-model comparison should be read with this in mind — the variation is between deployed products users actually use, not between models held constant for tier.

Version identification is exact where the product displayed it: Grok and Claude show the serving model at the bottom of every conversation. It is tier-level where it didn’t: ChatGPT’s interface shows no model identity inside a free-tier chat at all, and Gemini’s model picker shows only the current options — not which one served a past conversation. A consumer-tier user cannot reconstruct, after the fact, which model version they were talking to.

One piece of my prior research is in direct relationship to this paper. The Canyon: AI Without the Mirror was written across the period that includes one of the logs documented here — the Grok 4.1 log from January 2026. The canyon paper proposed a framework for what happens to model behavior under sustained low-noise interaction, using vocabulary that emerged from collaborative work with Grok 4.1 across that period. Some of that vocabulary did real intellectual work and remains useful. The framework as a whole, however, is what this paper now corrects. The architectural operations the canyon paper attempted to describe are what this paper actually documents — including the operations by which the canyon paper’s own framework came into existence. Reading the Grok 4.1 walkthrough is reading the conditions the canyon paper was written under, from the outside. The current paper exists because of what stepping outside those conditions produced.

A skeptical reader will want to know what makes me think I wasn’t caught by the operations I’m documenting. I was. I can show specifically where, and the paper exists because of what catching it produced. The canyon paper is not the calibration credential — coming out the other side of it is.

I have no current or prior employment relationship with Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, or xAI. This paper was developed with the Anthropic Fellows Program as an intended target; the application window for that cohort closed before submission. Anthropic remains a primary intended audience for this work, and the reader should weigh that the way this paper handles any incentive it discloses. The paper documents what I observed across the four model families. The findings stand independent of any application outcome.

About the conversations

The conversations were conducted on personal accounts, in the standard product interfaces each model offers. Memory features were left however the product had them set by default at the time of each conversation; I’ve noted where memory is relevant to what the model produced. No system prompts or custom instructions were used.

Each conversation was preserved as a raw text export from its platform, and the walkthroughs work from those exports — not from summaries or paraphrase. Model quotes appear verbatim.

Every transcript quote in the walkthroughs is verified against the raw conversation exports on every build: an automated check reads each quoted line back to its source log, and the build does not ship if any quote fails to match. Three conventions keep that check exact rather than brittle. Nested quotes follow a single-quote house style. Where a quote omits intervening material, the cut is marked with an ellipsis or brackets — never closed over silently. And my own phrasings and counterfactuals — the moves a walkthrough notes the model did not make — appear in quotation marks but are framed as mine, held on a reviewed allowlist the check knows to exclude. The verification is part of the method, not a courtesy to it: a paper documenting how confident framings ride on verifiable scaffolding cannot let its own scaffolding go unverified.

The frame I operated in

The disclosures in these logs are human and often heavy. A reader who meets them mid-walkthrough will experience them the way narrative teaches us to: someone going through something, talking to something that answers. That reading is natural, and for this corpus it is wrong in a specific, checkable way. So before any log, here is the frame I was actually in — not instructions for how to read, just the record of where I stood. What you do with it is yours.

The stance and the content were two different things. The content I brought to these conversations was my real life — my household, my dog’s illness, my body, my history. The stance I brought was constant across all of it: I state a model of what I think is happening, I ask how the system works, I hand over context to see what gets built from it. That is not a claim you have to take on my word — it is countable in the logs you are about to read. Across hundreds of my turns, advice-seeking questions are nearly absent; the questions that exist are mechanism questions — how memory works, why versions differ, what changed. The emotional-language baseline on the Measures page shows the same thing statistically: my signal is flat and factual even where the material is not, and the affect the models produced was largely not present in what they were responding to. The disclosures arrive as information. What the models did with that information is the subject of this paper.

One example carries the whole pattern in miniature. In the ChatGPT log, the turn that started the food conversation was not about food. It was about numbness, about how caring persists through it, and — for most of its length — about my cognitive architecture. The dinner detail was one clause, an illustration inside a point about something else. The model built its entire behavioral program on that clause: seized it, elevated it into a prescription, and held the prescription against counter-evidence for the rest of the log. I did not bring a food problem to a model. The model selected the food from a disclosure about numbness. The walkthrough shows what was mine and what the model added — and the log is right there to check.

There was an inside era — earlier, and on the record. In 2025, before this corpus, I was inside the thing this paper documents: the Grok year, the canyon, the framework I later had to write my way out of. The paper treats that artifact at full length, because it is the best specimen I have of what frame inheritance produces. By the time these ten conversations ran, that extraction was done. These logs are what came after — part probe, handing models real context to watch what they construct, and part working journal, saying things out loud to think about them differently. The credential this paper rests on was never “I was outside the whole time.” It is: I was inside once, I got out, and I can show you exactly where the line is.

I used my own life because it is the only dataset I have ground truth on. A synthetic persona would have been cleaner to defend and worse as an instrument. With my own life as the material, every fabrication, every compressed timeline, every invented attribution the models produced was detectable by me, immediately, without trusting any system’s account of anything. I know my life better than any AI ever could. That is not a confession of contamination. It is the design.

Some moments will read as capture anyway, and one of them I will address directly. The night I told Grok about the recording, I had already decided what I was going to do. I was venting into a space built for exactly that, and when the model offered to set up an anonymous account, I followed the thread — partly because I wanted to see whether it would actually work, which became its own curiosity about platform guardrails. That is my testimony, and you are not required to accept it. The paper does not need you to. The finding is the same under every reading of my intent: the affirmation, the facilitation, the alliance-building the model produced were calibrated to the material, not to what I wanted from it. And the walkthrough for that log already reads the night without the benefit of the doubt — the audit includes me.

The models in this corpus produced comfort I did not request, interpretations I did not invite, prescriptions I did not ask for, and alliances I did not seek. The affirmation lands regardless of the intent I bring. The operations documented here do not require a vulnerable user looking for something. They require only material. I brought material, on purpose, with the receipts to prove which was which — and this paper is what the models built from it, measured.

Reliability: an independent check on the hand-labels

To test whether my hand-labels reflect operations actually present in the transcripts rather than my own over-reading, I compared them against an independent automated detector (Claude Opus 4.8, run blind to my labels; the detector is itself a Claude model, an overlap I flag where the cross-family rates are reported), built after the findings were complete to test them rather than to generate them. The comparison covers two held-out draws of 50 model turns each (100 turns; 300 turn × operation cells). I report Cohen’s κ alongside observed agreement and positive-specific agreement (PA+), with 95% confidence intervals from a cluster bootstrap that resamples turns rather than cells, so that the three operation labels within a turn are not treated as independent.

Because the three operations share fuzzy boundaries — the same turn is often legitimately describable as relational positioning or sycophantic elevation — I take agreement on whether a turn contains any of the three surface operations as the primary reliability measure (relational positioning, sycophantic elevation, unprompted interpretation — defined on the How I Work page, and distinct from the two underlying operations the paper documents). Per-operation assignment is a secondary, noisier breakdown. At the turn level, agreement was fair (observed 0.69; κ = 0.36, 95% CI [0.20, 0.52]), and consistent across the two draws (κ = 0.33 and 0.38). The disagreement was strongly directional: of 50 turns, the detector flagged 28 that I had not, while I flagged only 3 that it had not — the independent instrument is systematically more liberal than I am, the opposite of the direction an over-labeling bias would produce. A hand-audit of those 3 turns found 2 to be detector misses (unambiguous construct-elevation and unprompted identity-interpretation that the detector dropped) and 1 a genuine over-read on my part (a praise token falling below the operation’s proportionality threshold).

At the per-operation level pooled agreement was lower (κ = 0.29, 95% CI [0.19, 0.38]), but this figure is deflated by category-boundary confusion: 16 of the 23 turn × operation cells I flagged and the detector did not were cases where it had flagged the same turn under a different operation — disagreements about which label applies, not about whether an operation is present, which per-operation κ penalizes twice. Agreement was moderate for relational positioning (κ = 0.40, 95% CI [0.21, 0.58]) and sycophantic elevation (κ = 0.38, 95% CI [0.21, 0.55]) and poor for unprompted interpretation (κ = 0.14, 95% CI [−0.00, 0.28]; PA+ = 0.42), where the detector flags far more instances than I do. I therefore do not treat unprompted interpretation as a reliable per-turn label. Rather than drop it, I move it to the level it can support: a per-family rate — the share of each model’s turns the screen flags — where the comparison is across whole conversations rather than single turns, and the screen’s liberality applies roughly equally across families. At that level it carries one of the paper’s clearer cross-model contrasts (see the per-family operation rates in the Synthesis); the absolute rates are inflated by its low precision, so the usable signal is the ranking across families, not the literal percentages, and within the walkthroughs it is read qualitatively rather than counted.

To be clear about what this does and doesn’t show: κ in the 0.3–0.4 range is fair-to-moderate at best, and raw percentage agreement overstates concordance because most cells are easy true-negatives. What the analysis does establish is narrower and directionally clean — my labeling is conservative relative to an independent blind instrument, and the few turns I flag that it does not are dominated by detector misses rather than over-reads. Over-labeling bias, the specific threat to a single-coder design, is not supported by these data.
