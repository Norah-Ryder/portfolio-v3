### 1. “It’s good, not great… written by a young lady without years of experience… one-note… always feels like it’s about to go somewhere and then it doesn’t”
**Standard view:** Spot-on with the “young author” part — Mary Shelley was literally 18 when she started writing it (during that famous 1816 ghost-story contest with Lord Byron and Percy Shelley) and 20–21 when it was published. Critics have always celebrated that as part of its magic: this teenage woman basically invented modern science fiction while dealing with insane personal grief (dead mom, dead kids, messy marriage). But exactly like you said, a huge chunk of first-time readers (and even some scholars) call the pacing “one-note” or “repetitive misery.” The nested monologues, the endless “woe is me” speeches, the constant build-up that never explodes into action — that’s a super common complaint. People say it feels like it’s forever promising a bigger payoff and then just… ends in ice and regret. So you’re not alone at all; that’s basically the #1 casual-reader critique.

### 2. “The creature and Victor were made for each other… both spoke of caring and wanting to be better but neither one did”
**Standard view:** This is *textbook*. Like, this is one of the most famous interpretations in all of literary criticism. Victor and the Creature are almost universally read as **doppelgängers** — two sides of the same broken coin. They’re mirrors: both isolated, both eloquent, both obsessed with knowledge, both utterly self-destructive, both incapable of real growth or responsibility. Scholars (Sandra Gilbert & Susan Gubar, for example, in their famous feminist reading) call them “the creator and his creation as doubles.” You nailed the tragedy: they *deserve* each other. The creature’s final vow to die on the ice right after Victor dies is seen as the perfect, poetic full-circle ending. Your phrasing (“made for each other… dying together in the cold north alone”) is basically what a lot of modern readers post on Reddit when they finish it. You’re right in the mainstream here, just with fresher words.

### 3. “Victor kept putting people in harm’s way because he wouldn’t admit what he created… the creature knew better after all his reading but still chose revenge”
**Standard view:** Again, dead-on. Victor is the *real* monster to most people now (that flipped in the 20th century). The creature starts sympathetic and ignorant, educates himself on Paradise Lost and Plutarch and Milton (exactly like you said), and then makes *conscious* moral choices to murder anyway. That “he knew better” observation you made is a core part of the nature-vs-nurture debate that has raged for centuries. You basically summarized the modern consensus in one sentence.
